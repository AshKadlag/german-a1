Why Marathi translations for German A1?

Open the Free Study Guide →

When you learn a new language, connecting new words to your mother tongue is faster than connecting them through a second language. If you look up das Wasser → "water" → "पाणी", you are doing two translations every time. Going directly from das Wasser → "पाणी" locks it in much more quickly.

English is not the mother tongue of most Indian students. Marathi is. Yet almost zero German learning tools acknowledge this — they assume you are either a native English speaker or a German speaker already. There is nothing in between for us.

There are tens of thousands of Marathi speakers in Germany, Austria, and Switzerland — engineers, nurses, students on APS scholarships and university programs — and most of them are learning German from English-language textbooks. This guide is an attempt to close that gap at A1 level, where vocabulary is the biggest hurdle.

A1 is where you prove to yourself you can learn this language. It takes real, dedicated effort — there is no shortcut. But if your study materials are fighting against how your brain naturally works, you are making the climb harder than it needs to be. This guide at least removes that one unnecessary friction.

What is German A1, and what vocabulary do you need?

German A1 is the lowest level of the Common European Framework of Reference (CEFR). It covers basic everyday communication: greetings, introductions, numbers, directions, shopping, food, and writing a short letter. It is the exam most immigrants are required to pass for a family reunification visa, and the first step on the path to permanent residency for many.

For the A1 exam, you need to handle four skills:

  • Hören (Listening) — understanding short spoken announcements and dialogues
  • Lesen (Reading) — reading short messages, signs, and notices
  • Schreiben (Writing) — writing a short formal or informal letter or note
  • Sprechen (Speaking) — introducing yourself and responding to simple questions

The vocabulary needed for all four skills fits in roughly 300–400 words. The list below covers the 300 most important ones, grouped by topic.

How to study effectively for A1

Open the Free Study Guide →

Having the right resources only matters if you use them consistently. A1 requires real, dedicated learning — the grammar is manageable, but the vocabulary has to be drilled until it is automatic. Here is a schedule that worked for me:

1

Week 1 — Greetings, Introduction, Numbers

Start with the three categories you will use in the Sprechen section. Go through every card once reading both sides, then flip through testing yourself. Aim to know these 70 words before anything else — they appear in every part of the exam.

2

Weeks 1–3 — 2 to 3 categories per day

Work through the remaining 19 categories steadily. Question words and modal verbs are high priority — they appear in almost every sentence. Food, travel, and shopping come up repeatedly in the Lesen section.

3

Daily — 10 minutes of Hören

Open Exam Practice → Hören. Hit play and listen before reading the text. Your ear adjusts to German sounds faster than you expect with daily exposure. Even 10 minutes a day compounds significantly over three weeks.

4

2 weeks before the exam — Letter structures

Open the Schreiben tab. Memorise the opening, body structure, and closing for both formal and informal letters. The exam prompt is always short — if you know the skeleton, you fill in the details in under 10 minutes.

5

Final week — Speak out loud every day

This one feels uncomfortable at first, but it is the most important step. Work through the Sprechen cards out loud. Record yourself on your phone and play it back — you will catch pronunciation issues you completely miss while speaking.
